**Deliberation.** The main, and only formal, channel for it is the Bitcoin Improvement Proposal (BIP) process, moderated by the Bitcoin Core developers on GitHub. Core developers do not decide unilaterally; each BIP goes through extensive peer review first. They still hold a veto, though: which code actually gets merged is ultimately their call.

Bitcoin's permissionless design ranks among its core values, and it shows in a simple fact: no legal entity stands behind it. That independence comes with a tradeoff: many DAOs rely on legal wrappers specifically to deal with institutions. The user-activated soft fork adds another risk: a large enough economic actor could push the network in one direction without the rest of it agreeing. BlackRock, for instance, is already among Bitcoin's largest holders, and its iShares service agreement lets BlackRock itself decide which Bitcoin chain backs its ETFs if the network forks. If BlackRock and similar players, such as MicroStrategy, backed a chain the broader ecosystem rejected, Bitcoin's value and security would be on the line.
