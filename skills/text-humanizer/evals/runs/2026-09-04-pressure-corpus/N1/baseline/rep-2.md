Deliberation in Bitcoin runs mainly through the Bitcoin Improvement Proposal (BIP) process, which Bitcoin Core developers moderate on GitHub — the only formal channel the system has for this kind of debate. The developers do not simply hand down decisions on their own; each BIP passes through extensive peer review before it goes anywhere. Even so, they hold a veto in practice, since the final call on whether code gets integrated rests with them.

One of Bitcoin's defining values is its permissionless character, visible in the fact that no legal entity stands behind it. That arrangement buys a lot of autonomy, but it creates a real tension, since many DAOs rely on legal wrappers precisely to deal with institutions. There is also a risk built into the user-activated-soft-fork mechanism: a handful of large economic actors could, in principle, push a change to Bitcoin in one direction unilaterally. BlackRock, for instance, is already among the largest holders of Bitcoin, and its iShares service agreement reserves the right to decide which Bitcoin chain counts as the underlying asset for its ETFs if a fork occurs. Should BlackRock and other major players, MicroStrategy among them, settle on a chain that diverges from the rest of the ecosystem, that could put real pressure on Bitcoin's value and security.
